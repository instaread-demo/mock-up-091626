Osteoporosis develops when bone mineral density and bone mass decrease, or when the structure and strength of bone changes, and it is often called a silent disease because there are usually no symptoms until a bone breaks. Fractures can happen in any bone, but they are most common in the hip, the vertebrae of the spine, and the wrist, according to MedlinePlus and the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases.

It is common enough that CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics put the age-adjusted prevalence at 12.6 percent of adults aged 50 and over, with another 43.1 percent carrying low bone mass, a precursor to the condition.

None of that is something a mattress fixes. What a mattress can do is change the mechanics of the eight or so hours you spend on it and the two transfers at either end of the night. That is why this guide weights profile height, edge support, and surface responsiveness more heavily than it would on a cooling or budget page.

NIAMS advises people with osteoporosis to avoid movements that twist the spine or bend it forward from the waist, and a mattress that lets you roll over in one motion rather than push, twist, and reset is doing real work inside that guidance.

There is a second reason to take the sleep surface seriously, and it runs in the other direction. A growing body of research links sleep disruption to bone health. How I Tested and Ranked the Best Mattresses For Osteoporosis

I tested more than 50 mattresses using the same framework, and fourteen made this list. Every bed here was evaluated in the side, back, and stomach positions, at the perimeter, and with a partner moving on the far side of a queen.

What I tested for

  • Value: I weighed what each bed delivered on the other five dimensions against its queen price after standard promotions, not its list price.
  • Edge Support: I sat on the perimeter the way you would to put on shoes, stood up from that seated position, then lay along the outer six inches. I noted how far the edge compressed under me, whether the surface stayed level or tilted toward the floor, and whether standing up took a hand on the mattress to brace against.
  • Trial Period: I compared the length of each trial, the return terms, and whether the mattress can be returned at all.
  • Response Time: I rolled from back to side and side to stomach and counted how many motions it took, and whether the surface came back up under me or held an impression.
  • Motion Transfer: I lay still while my partner changed positions and got in and out on the far side of a queen, and noted how much of that reached my side.
  • Cooling and Breathability: I compared surface temperature by touch through the first hours against the other beds tested in the same session. Cooling results here are comparative and observed, not guarantees.
  • Pressure relief and spinal alignment: the two qualitative dimensions the copy leans on. I noted where pressure concentrated at the hip and shoulder, and whether my hips stayed level with my shoulders in each position.

How the scores work

Each mattress earns a score from 0 to 10 on six dimensions: Value, Edge Support, Trial Period, Response Time, Motion Transfer, and Cooling and Breathability. The overall score is the average of the six, rounded to one decimal. The AS3’s 9.2, for example, is the average of 9, 8, 9, 9, 10, and 10. A mattress that cannot be returned scores 0 on Trial Period, which is why the Westin Heavenly Bed carries the lowest overall score on this page.

What mattered most for this category

  • Profile height and ease of transfer. A bed that sits near sitting height is easier to get onto and off of than one you drop into or climb up to. Profile height sits in every spec box on this page for that reason, and it is called out in every product section.
  • Edge support. The perimeter is where you sit to put on shoes and where you push up from in the morning. A perimeter that collapses turns a routine transfer into a scramble, so edge support carries more weight here than on most guides.
  • Responsiveness. Turning over on a slow surface means pushing and twisting. A surface that recovers quickly lets you roll in one motion instead.
  • Pressure relief at the hip and shoulder without losing the hip line. The hip is both the joint that needs cushioning on the side and the one that must not sink out of alignment. Beds that solved only half of that did not make the list.

How the winner was chosen

The category winner is the bed that performed best for this specific shopper intent, not the bed with the highest aggregate score. Two picks on this page, the Zoma Boost and the Nolah Evolution, tie at 9.5 and both outscore the Amerisleep AS3 at 9.2.

The AS3 still wins the category, because it was the most positionally versatile bed I tested and its 12 inch profile pairs more easily with a tall frame or foundation than the 14 and 15 inch picks here. This feature matters more here than it would on, say, a best cooling page.

Scores are never adjusted to fit a ranking. Where a pick trails on a dimension, that shows up in its cons instead.

Why you can trust these rankings

Every bed on this page was tested by me, in person, against the same framework, and every pick carries a stated limitation and a fit-based alternative from this same list. You can read the full process on our testing methodology page.

What Osteoporosis Is, and Who Gets It

Osteoporosis is a bone disease that develops when bone mineral density and bone mass decrease, or when the structure and strength of bone changes, which raises the risk of fractures. NIAMS calls it a silent disease because there are typically no symptoms until a bone breaks, and notes that bones affected by osteoporosis can fracture from a minor fall or from normal stresses such as bending, lifting, or coughing.

Anyone can develop it, and risk rises with age. MedlinePlus lists sex, age, body size, family history, hormone changes, diet, long-term use of certain medicines, and several medical conditions among the factors that increase risk.

The FDA Office of Women’s Health notes that of the estimated 10 million Americans with osteoporosis, more than 8 million are women, and that in the United States the condition affects one in four women aged 65 or older.

Womenshealth.gov covers prevention across the lifespan, which starts long before anyone is shopping for a mattress.

The current national numbers come from CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics. Its 2021 data brief put the age-adjusted prevalence of osteoporosis at the femur neck or lumbar spine at 12.6 percent of adults aged 50 and over, 19.6 percent among women and 4.4 percent among men, with low bone mass affecting another 43.1 percent.

Current summary figures are maintained at CDC FastStats, and osteoporosis remains a tracked objective under Healthy People.

Screening, diagnosis, and treatment are your doctor’s territory, not a mattress guide’s. NIAMS bone health resources and the MedlinePlus magazine overview are good places to read further before that appointment.

The Sleep and Bone Health Connection

Research over the past decade has looked at whether sleep itself relates to bone health, and the pattern is consistent enough to mention, with the caveat that most of it is observational.

A 2021 review in Current Opinion in Endocrinology, Diabetes and Obesity summarized the evidence that sleep and circadian disruptions, including night shift work, abnormal sleep durations, and obstructive sleep apnea, appear detrimental to skeletal health, while stating plainly that more research is needed on which disturbances matter most and why.

A cross-sectional NHANES analysis published in Scientific Reports found that women over 50 reporting less than five hours of sleep per day had substantially higher odds of osteoporosis than other groups.

A study of 3,659 postmenopausal women in Archives of Osteoporosis found higher osteoporosis risk associated with total and nocturnal sleep durations of six hours or less, and with taking daytime naps. A 2016 study in Sleep Medicine reported that poor sleep quality, and sleep disturbances in particular, were associated with bone stiffness index.

More recently, a 2025 analysis of the ELSA and HRS cohorts found that participants with persistently poor sleep quality had significantly higher osteoporosis incidence than those with consistently good sleep.

And in August 2026, an international panel published an expert consensus in Bone Research arguing that sleep disorder management should be integrated into osteoporosis prevention and care pathways, with recommendations that include screening fall history and optimizing the home environment for fall risk, such as removing tripping hazards and providing enough light for getting up at night.

This body of research suggests that sleeping poorly is worth addressing with your doctor for reasons beyond how you feel the next day. A mattress does not change any of that. What it can change is how comfortable you are and how easily you get in and out of bed, and that is the only claim this guide makes.

How to Choose a Mattress if You Have Osteoporosis

Bed height and ease of transfer

The number that gets ignored on most mattress pages is the one that matters most here. Your mattress profile and your frame or foundation combine into a single surface height, and that bed height determines whether getting into and out of bed is a step or a climb.

The general target is a surface where you can sit on the edge with your feet flat on the floor and your knees at roughly hip level. Too low and you are pushing up from a deep squat. Too high and you are sliding down to reach the floor. Measure from the floor to the top of your current mattress, decide whether that number is working, and then shop profile height accordingly.

On this page, profiles run from 11.5 inches (Saatva Classic, low profile option) to 15 inches (Zoma Boost, Organica Plush, and the Nolah Evolution with its pillow top upgrade), and three picks let you set height at the point of order: the Saatva Classic through its two profile heights, the Westin Heavenly Bed through its two box spring heights, and the Nolah Evolution through its choice of cover.

Edge support

The perimeter is where you sit down, where you swing your legs over, and where you push up from. An edge that compresses under you turns a controlled movement into an uncontrolled one.

Reinforced coil perimeters and high-density foam rails hold up better than an unreinforced foam edge, which is why nine of the picks on this page score a 10 on edge support, while the two all-foam models come in at 8 and 9.

Test it yourself when your mattress arrives: sit on the edge as you normally would, then stand up. If the surface tips toward the floor or you find yourself needing a hand on the mattress, that is the bed telling you something before the trial period ends.

Firmness and spinal alignment

The goal is a surface that keeps your spine in the same neutral line it holds when you are standing well. Too soft, and your hips drop below your shoulders and you have to work to get out. Too firm, and your shoulder and hip carry your weight at two small points instead of spreading it.

Most sleepers land in the medium to medium-firm range, but firmness is a function of your body weight and your sleeping position, not a universal number.

Lighter-weight sleepers experience any given bed as firmer than heavier sleepers do, so a lighter side sleeper often needs a softer surface than the standard recommendation, and a heavier sleeper often needs a firmer one to avoid sinking past the comfort layers.

If you are not sure, the picks here that come in three firmness levels give you the most room to be wrong once.

Responsiveness

NIAMS advises people with osteoporosis to avoid movements that twist the spine or bend it forward from the waist. That guidance points at a mattress property most shoppers never think about.

On a slow, deep surface, turning over means pushing off with an arm and rotating your torso out of an impression. On a responsive surface, you roll in one motion. The innerspring and coil-forward picks on this page recover fastest; the soft memory foam picks are slowest.

Motion isolation

If you share a bed, a partner getting up at night is one of the most common reasons people wake. The picks with a deep memory foam comfort system isolate movement best as a mattress for two, while the thinner-comfort hybrids transfer the most.

Adjustable bases

Raising the head of the bed makes it easier to get into a seated position, and slightly raising the legs takes pressure off the lower back. If you use one, confirm the mattress is compatible before ordering, and check for gaps between the mattress and the base in raised positions.

Whether an adjustable base is right for you is a question worth putting to your doctor or physical therapist rather than a mattress reviewer.

Sleeping Positions and Pillow Setup

Back sleeping distributes your weight most evenly and makes a neutral spine easiest to hold. A pillow under the knees takes some strain off the lower back. Your head pillow should fill the space between a mattress for back sleeping and your head without pushing your chin toward your chest.

Side sleeping works well on a surface that lets the shoulder and hip settle while keeping the spine level. A pillow lengthwise between the knees keeps the top leg from pulling the hips out of alignment. Your head pillow needs to be tall enough to fill the gap between your ear and the surface of a mattress for side sleeping. And this means a taller pillow than a back sleeper needs.

Stomach sleeping is generally not recommended with osteoporosis, since it extends the spine and turns the neck to one side for hours. If it is the only position you can fall asleep in, a firmer surface that keeps the hips from sinking is the least bad version of it. Still, it is worth raising with your provider than just quietly buying a mattress for stomach sleeping.

Movements to avoid, per NIAMS, include twisting the spine and bending forward from the waist. That applies to getting out of bed too: rolling to your side, dropping your legs off the edge, and pushing up with your arms is a gentler sequence than sitting straight up from flat.

Setting Up the Bedroom

The Bone Research consensus panel recommends optimizing the home environment to reduce fall risk and support sleep at the same time, including removing tripping hazards and providing enough light to move safely at night. NIAMS makes similar points in its guidance on living with osteoporosis.

Here are the practical steps to make:

  • Keep a clear path from the bed to the bathroom and light it well enough to use at night.
  • Secure or remove loose rugs near the bed, and keep cords out of the walking path.
  • Keep shoes with support and thin nonslip soles near the bed rather than walking in socks.
  • Keep a phone within reach of the bed.
  • Make sure there is a stable surface, a nightstand or a bed frame, near where you sit up.

FAQs

What is the best mattress firmness if you have osteoporosis?

Most people do best somewhere in the medium to medium-firm range, because that band tends to cushion the hips and shoulders while still holding the spine in a neutral line and staying easy to get out of.

The right number for you depends on your sleeping position and body weight. Side sleepers usually need something softer than back sleepers, and lighter-weight sleepers usually need something softer than heavier sleepers. Talk with your doctor or physical therapist about your own situation.

Is a firm mattress good for osteoporosis?

Not necessarily. A very firm surface concentrates your weight at the hip and the shoulder rather than spreading it, which is uncomfortable for most side sleepers. A very soft surface has the opposite problem: you sink in and getting up takes more effort.

Neither extreme is a good default, which is why most of the picks on this page sit in the middle of the firmness scale or offer a choice.

Does mattress height matter if you have osteoporosis?

It matters more than most other specs. Bed height determines whether you sit down onto the surface or drop onto it, and whether you stand up from it or slide off it.

Aim for a setup where you can sit on the edge with your feet flat on the floor and your knees at roughly hip level, and remember that mattress profile and frame height combine to produce that number. If your current bed is close but not right, changing the foundation is often easier than changing the mattress.

Can a mattress help with osteoporosis?

A mattress does not treat osteoporosis, change bone density, or prevent a fracture. What it can do is affect your comfort, how well your spine is supported for the hours you are on it, and how much effort it takes to get in and out of bed. Those are worth optimizing on their own terms. Any decision about managing the condition itself belongs with your healthcare provider.

What does the American Chiropractic Association endorsement mean?

Four picks in this guide (Amerisleep AS3, Organica Plush, Amerisleep AS6 Black Series, and Amerisleep AS5) carry an endorsement from the American Chiropractic Association. The endorsement reflects support and build quality as judged by licensed chiropractors. It is not a medical claim that a mattress treats, prevents, or improves osteoporosis.

Is memory foam or a hybrid better for osteoporosis?

Hybrids tend to suit this situation better on two dimensions that matter here: they usually have reinforced perimeters that hold up when you sit on the edge, and their coil support recovers faster, so turning over takes less effort.

Memory foam usually wins on motion isolation and on deep cushioning at the shoulder. If getting in and out of bed is your priority, look at the hybrids on this page. If a partner’s movement is what wakes you, look at the foam builds.

Is an adjustable base worth it?

For many people, raising the head of the bed makes getting into a seated position easier, and slightly raising the legs eases pressure on the lower back. Whether it is right for you is a question for your doctor or physical therapist. If you buy one, check that your mattress is rated for it and look for gaps between the mattress and the base in raised positions.

Does poor sleep affect bone health?

Research has found associations between sleep and bone health, though most of it is observational and the mechanisms are still being studied. But none of that means a mattress treats or prevents the condition, and none of it replaces medical advice.

What sleeping position is best if you have osteoporosis?

Back sleeping distributes weight most evenly and makes a neutral spine easiest to maintain, usually with a pillow under the knees. Side sleeping works well with a pillow between the knees to keep the hips level. Stomach sleeping is generally not recommended because it extends the spine and holds the neck turned.

NIAMS also advises avoiding movements that twist the spine or bend it forward from the waist, which is worth applying to how you get out of bed as well as how you sleep in it.

How often should a mattress be replaced?

Most mattresses lose support somewhere in the seven to ten year range, sooner if they were inexpensive to begin with. If you can see a dip where you sleep, if you wake up feeling less supported than when you lay down, or if you have started avoiding one side of the bed, those are the practical signals.

What pillow works best with osteoporosis?

The pillow’s job is to fill the space between your head and the mattress so your neck stays in line with the rest of your spine. Side sleepers need more loft than back sleepers, since the gap is larger. A pillow that pushes your chin toward your chest is too tall, and one that lets your head tip back is too short.

Did You Find Your Next Bed?

If you take one thing from this guide, make it the height measurement. Before you order anything, measure from the floor to the top of your current mattress and decide whether that number is working for you, because it is the variable that decides how the first and last minute of every night go, and it is the one you can control at the point of purchase by pairing the right profile with the right frame.

After that, work down the list: a perimeter that holds when you sit on it, a surface that keeps your hips level with your shoulders in the position you actually sleep in, and enough responsiveness that turning over takes one motion instead of a push and a twist. Every bed on this page was picked against those three things, in that order.

Use the trial. Sit on the edge, stand up, roll over, and get out of bed the way you actually do it, in the room where the bed lives. That is the test no spec sheet answers, and thirteen of the fourteen picks here give you at least 100 nights to run it.
