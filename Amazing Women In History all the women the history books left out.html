Abigail Scott Duniway, Pioneer for Women’s Suffrage in the Pacific Northwest

Abigail Scott Duniway (1834–1915) dedicated her life to advocating for women’s rights, particularly suffrage, across Oregon, Washington, and Idaho. Her relentless efforts leading regional campaigns earned her recognition as the primary architect of women’s suffrage in the Pacific Northwest. Her story illustrates unwavering determination against entrenched legal restrictions and social hostility.

Early Life & Background

Abigail Jane Scott was born on October 22, 1834, in Groveland, Illinois. In 1852, her family journeyed westward on the Oregon Trail, a 2,000-mile trek marked by cholera outbreaks, extreme weather, and exhausting labor. Tragedy struck en route when her mother died from cholera, forcing 17-year-old Abigail to take over cooking, washing, and caring for her younger siblings for the remainder of the journey.

Upon settling in Oregon, Abigail married Benjamin C. Duniway in 1853 and began managing a farm and family in Clackamas County. When Benjamin was permanently disabled in a runaway team accident, Abigail became the sole breadwinner for her family of six children.

She opened a millinery and dressmaking shop in Albany, Oregon, where she listened to stories from her female clientele. Married women recounted how Oregon’s legal framework (specifically coverture laws) allowed husbands to legally control their wives’ earnings, seize their personal property, and claim sole guardianship of their children. These personal accounts directly fueled her commitment to legal reform.

The Power of the Pen

In 1871, Abigail moved her family to Portland and founded The New Northwest, a weekly newspaper dedicated to women’s rights and human equality that ran for 16 years. At a time when mainstream media routinely mocked female activists, her newspaper provided a vital platform to advocate for:

  • Legal Equality: Repealing coverture laws so married women could own property and retain their own wages.
  • Educational Access: Opening state universities and professional schools to female students.
  • Employment Rights: Securing equal pay for female teachers and working women.

Running the newspaper posed severe financial and social challenges. Anti-suffrage opponents organized boycotts against businesses that bought advertising in The New Northwest. Subscriptions were frequently canceled, forcing Abigail to take on extra print job contracts to keep the press funded.

Social pushback was equally intense. Local clergy used pulpits to denounce her as a threat to the traditional Christian family, while rival male editors published editorials calling her “unfeminine” and “strident.” During a lecture tour in Jacksonville, Oregon, hostile crowds threw rotten eggs and burned her in effigy.

Pioneering Suffrage Campaigns

Abigail led vigorous suffrage campaigns throughout Oregon, Washington, and Idaho. She traveled thousands of miles by stagecoach, steamship, and horseback—often traveling alone to rural mining camps and farming towns despite social expectations that respectable women remain homebound.

Her campaign strategy relied on specific, persuasive arguments tailored to her frontier audiences:

  • Taxation Without Representation: Tax-paying female landowners who managed ranches and farms deserved a vote on local tax assessments.
  • Equal Responsibility, Equal Rights: Women endured the same severe hardships of pioneer life as men and held equal stake in town laws.
  • Non-Partisan Campaigning: Suffrage should focus strictly on equal rights and avoid alienating male voters with prohibition ties.

Her advocacy achieved its first major triumph in Idaho. In 1896, after Abigail toured the state holding rallies and lobbying state legislators, Idaho passed a constitutional amendment granting women the vote, making it the fourth U.S. state to equalize voting rights.

Setbacks & Political Resistance

Despite her success in Idaho, Abigail’s campaign in Oregon endured decades of political defeats. Oregon male voters rejected women’s suffrage referendums five separate times: in 1884, 1900, 1906, 1908, and 1910.

These failed referendums reflected deep-seated institutional opposition:

  • Liquor Industry Lobbies: Saloon owners and liquor distributors feared that female voters would enact prohibition laws, leading the industry to fund anti-suffrage newspaper campaigns and pay voters to reject the amendments.
  • Political Machine Opposition: Established party bosses opposed suffrage out of fear that female voters would disrupt existing patronage networks and demand anti-corruption reforms.
  • Internal Movement Friction: National leaders like Susan B. Anthony favored aggressive national rallies, whereas Abigail insisted on localized, non-confrontational lobbying designed to persuade male frontier voters without alienating them.

A Historic Victory

Abigail’s persistence finally bore fruit during the 1912 Oregon election. After organizing a targeted campaign across every county in the state, Oregon voters approved the equal suffrage amendment on November 5, 1912.

In recognition of her forty-year campaign, Governor Oswald West invited the 78-year-old activist to draft Oregon’s Equal Suffrage Proclamation. On November 30, 1912, Abigail signed the official proclamation alongside the Governor. She then became the first woman registered to vote in Multnomah County.

Legacy & Impact

Abigail Scott Duniway’s legacy spans the legal and political foundation of the American West. By establishing a dedicated regional press, lobbying territorial and state legislatures, and organizing local suffrage leagues, she brought women’s political rights from a fringe concept into the mainstream legal framework.

Her work directly contributed to enfranchising women across Idaho (1896), Washington (1910), and Oregon (1912), establishing the Pacific Northwest as an early stronghold for universal suffrage years before the passage of the 19th Amendment in 1920.
