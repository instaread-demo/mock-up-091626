How AI Is Changing the Way Video Games Are Made

By jayisgames.com | September 14, 2026

Artificial intelligence has been part of video games for decades. Enemies deciding when to attack, computer-controlled racers reacting to your moves, and NPCs choosing where to wander all depend on some form of game AI.
But the newest generation of artificial intelligence is doing something very different.

Instead of simply controlling what happens inside a game, AI is increasingly helping developers actually create the game itself. From artwork and dialogue to level design, testing, and coding, new tools are giving developers ways to build games faster--and potentially attempt projects that once would have required much larger teams.

For indie developers in particular, that could be a major change.

Smaller Teams Can Suddenly Do More


Making a game usually involves a long list of specialized skills. Someone needs to program it. Someone needs to create artwork. There may be animation, music, sound effects, dialogue, level design, testing, marketing art, and dozens of other jobs before a game ever reaches players.

A small indie studio may only have a handful of people trying to do all of those things.

AI tools can potentially fill some of the gaps.
A programmer who isn't an artist might use an image generator to explore ideas for environments or characters. A developer could use an AI coding assistant to help identify an error or quickly create a prototype. Generative tools can also produce placeholder dialogue, concept art, item descriptions, textures, and other materials while a game is still being developed.

That doesn't necessarily mean the AI-generated version becomes part of the finished game. Sometimes simply being able to create a rough version quickly can help developers figure out whether an idea is worth pursuing.

Games Could Become More Responsive


AI may also change what happens once we start playing.

Most game dialogue is traditionally written in advance. Even in a huge role-playing game, players are ultimately selecting from conversations and responses that developers anticipated beforehand.

Generative AI introduces the possibility of characters that can respond more freely.

Imagine walking into a fantasy village and asking a shopkeeper a completely unexpected question. Instead of giving you one of five prerecorded responses, the character could potentially generate an answer based on its personality, location, knowledge, and events in the game world.

That could make games feel significantly less scripted.
Of course, it also presents a giant design problem. Game developers usually want tight control over their worlds. A character that can say almost anything is considerably harder to manage than one with carefully written dialogue.

AI Can Help Build Bigger Worlds


Procedural generation is nothing new to gaming. Developers have long used algorithms to generate maps, dungeons, terrain, loot, and other content.

Generative AI could push that idea much further.

Rather than simply arranging predefined rooms differently, future systems might help generate buildings, quests, characters, conversations, or environmental details dynamically.

For indie games, the possibilities are especially interesting. A developer might be able to create the feeling of a much larger game world without manually designing every single object inside it.
The challenge will be making those worlds interesting rather than simply enormous.

Players generally don't care that a game contains 10,000 quests if 9,900 of them feel repetitive. Good game design still requires someone to decide what is fun.

There Is an Authenticity Question


AI-generated content is also creating a debate that extends far beyond games.

If artwork, dialogue, music, or code can be generated almost instantly, players may increasingly wonder how something was created.

The same thing is already happening with online writing, where tools such as an AI detector free service can be used to examine whether text may have been generated by artificial intelligence.

Games could face similar questions.
Was that character written by a person? Was the background art illustrated by an artist? Was the soundtrack composed traditionally or generated from a prompt?

For some players, none of that may matter if the game is good. Others may care considerably, especially when a game's appeal comes from its artwork, storytelling, or distinctive creative style.

Transparency could therefore become an increasingly important part of how developers talk about their games.

AI Could Make Game Testing Faster


One of the less flashy applications of AI may ultimately be one of the most useful: finding things that are broken.
Games can contain an incredible number of possible interactions. Players inevitably climb somewhere they weren't supposed to climb, combine items developers never expected them to combine, or discover a spectacular new way to break a level.

Automated systems can simulate player behavior repeatedly and help identify bugs, balance problems, and unusual situations.

This could be particularly valuable for smaller studios that don't have enormous quality-assurance teams.
Instead of replacing human testers, AI could help them investigate a much larger number of possibilities before release.

The Developer Still Matters Most


AI can generate an image, suggest a line of dialogue, or help produce code. What it cannot automatically provide is a great reason to play a game.

Some of the most memorable indie games became successful precisely because someone had a strange, clever, or deeply personal idea and turned it into something players hadn't experienced before.

That creativity is unlikely to become less valuable as AI tools improve. It may become more valuable.
If almost anyone can generate technically competent assets, the games that stand out will be the ones with great concepts, clever mechanics, memorable characters, and developers who know exactly what kind of experience they want to create.

AI may dramatically change the tools used to make games.
But hitting "generate" and making a game worth playing are still two very different things.
